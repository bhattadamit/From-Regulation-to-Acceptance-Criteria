Business requirements: Regulation E consumer liability for unauthorized electronic fund transfers
Initiative: Regulation E – Unauthorized Transfer Consumer Liability
Platform: Consumer Disputes Management Platform
Regulatory reference: 12 CFR § 1005.6 – Liability of consumer for unauthorized transfers
Business domain: Deposit Operations / Electronic Funds Transfer Disputes
Primary users: Disputes analysts, claims operations, compliance officers, and consumers
1. Business objective
Implement a rules-driven consumer liability assessment capability in the disputes platform to determine the consumer's potential financial liability for unauthorized electronic fund transfers (EFTs), consistent with Regulation E, 12 CFR § 1005.6.

The platform must:
1. Identify the applicable consumer liability framework based on the type and timing of the unauthorized transfer.
2. Calculate the consumer's potential liability using the regulatory notification timeframes.
3. Apply the appropriate liability limits.
4. Account for subsequent unauthorized transfers occurring after the consumer notifies the bank.
5. Distinguish unauthorized transfers from transfers made by a consumer or another person with actual authority.
6. Capture the evidence, dates, calculations, and rationale supporting the liability determination.
7. Support regulatory exceptions, including circumstances where the consumer's delay in notification was caused by extenuating circumstances.
8. Prevent consumer liability from being assessed when the bank's failure to provide required disclosures or periodic statements affects the applicability of the liability rules.
Important implementation principle: The platform should calculate the maximum permissible consumer liability under Regulation E, not automatically conclude that the consumer must pay that amount. The bank may adopt more favorable consumer liability policies.
2. Regulatory rules to be implemented
The following summarizes the primary requirements of 12 CFR § 1005.6.
Scenario	Maximum consumer liability
Consumer reports loss or theft of an access device within 2 business days after learning of the loss/theft	Lesser of $50 or unauthorized transfers occurring before notification
Consumer fails to report loss/theft within 2 business days	Lesser of $500 or the sum of applicable amounts under the regulatory calculation
Consumer does not report an unauthorized EFT appearing on a periodic statement within 60 calendar days after the statement is sent	Potential liability for qualifying subsequent transfers occurring after the 60-day period and before notification
Unauthorized transfer occurs after notification to the financial institution	Consumer generally has no liability for that subsequent transfer
Extenuating circumstances prevent timely notification	Applicable notification period must be extended reasonably

Important distinction: The two-business-day rule and the 60-calendar-day rule are separate tests. The first concerns loss or theft of an access device. The second concerns failure to report unauthorized EFTs reflected on periodic statements.

The $500 calculation is not simply a flat $500 charge. It must account for the applicable $50 initial liability component, additional transfers occurring within the prescribed timeframe, and the regulatory limitations.

The platform should also account for the statutory exception concerning the institution's failure to provide required disclosures or periodic statements.
3. Epic and user stories
EPIC REGE-001: Determine consumer liability for unauthorized EFTs
Epic description: As a financial institution, we need to calculate consumer liability for unauthorized EFTs using the applicable Regulation E notification rules so that dispute outcomes are accurate, consistent, explainable, and compliant.

US-001: Identify whether a transaction qualifies as an unauthorized EFT
User story

As a disputes analyst, I want the platform to determine whether a disputed transaction meets the Regulation E definition of an unauthorized EFT so that consumer liability rules are applied only to eligible transactions.

Business rules
- The transfer must meet the definition of an unauthorized EFT under 12 CFR § 1005.2(m).
- The transfer must have been initiated by someone other than the consumer.
- The person initiating the transfer must not have had actual authority.
- The consumer must not have received a benefit from the transfer.
- Transfers initiated by a person to whom the consumer furnished an access device are subject to the applicable authorization rules.
- Transfers made by persons with actual authority are not treated as unauthorized merely because the consumer disputes them.
Acceptance criteria – Gherkin
Feature: Identify unauthorized electronic fund transfers

  Scenario: Transaction initiated by an unauthorized third party
    Given a consumer disputes an electronic fund transfer
    And the transfer was initiated by another person
    And that person did not have actual authority
    And the consumer received no benefit from the transfer
    When the platform evaluates the disputed transaction
    Then the transaction should be classified as an unauthorized EFT
    And the transaction should be eligible for Regulation E liability assessment

  Scenario: Transaction initiated by an authorized person
    Given a consumer disputes an electronic fund transfer
    And the transfer was initiated by a person with actual authority
    When the platform evaluates the disputed transaction
    Then the transaction should not be classified as an unauthorized EFT
    And Regulation E unauthorized transfer liability should not be applied

  Scenario: Consumer received a benefit from the transfer
    Given a consumer disputes an electronic fund transfer
    And the consumer received a benefit from the transfer
    When the platform evaluates the disputed transaction
    Then the transaction should not qualify as an unauthorized EFT
    And the platform should record the reason for exclusion

  Scenario: Authorization status cannot be determined
    Given a consumer disputes an electronic fund transfer
    And the available evidence is insufficient to determine actual authority
    When the platform evaluates the disputed transaction
    Then the platform should flag the transaction for manual review
    And the platform should not automatically assign consumer liability

US-003: Apply the $50 consumer liability limit
User story

As a disputes analyst, I want the platform to calculate consumer liability when loss or theft of an access device is reported within two business days so that the consumer is not assessed more than the amount permitted by Regulation E.

Business rules

Under 12 CFR § 1005.6(b)(1), consumer liability is limited to the lesser of:
- $50; or
- The amount of unauthorized transfers occurring before the institution is notified.
The bank may apply a lower liability amount under its own policy.

US-004: Apply the $500 consumer liability framework when notification is delayed
User story

As a disputes analyst, I want the platform to calculate liability when the consumer fails to notify the institution within two business days of learning about access device loss or theft so that the applicable Regulation E liability limit is correctly applied.

Business rules

Under 12 CFR § 1005.6(b)(2), the maximum liability is the lesser of $500 or the sum of:
1. The lesser of $50 or the amount of unauthorized transfers occurring within the first two-business-day period; and
2. The amount of qualifying unauthorized transfers occurring after that period and before notification, provided the institution establishes that those transfers would not have occurred had timely notification been given.
The institution must retain the basis for applying the additional liability component.

    And the maximum permissible liability should not exceed $500

    And additional qualifying unauthorized transfers total $100

    Then the maximum permissible consumer liability should be $140

  Scenario: Total qualifying liability exceeds $500
    Given the initial liability component is $50
    And additional qualifying unauthorized transfers total $600

  Scenario: Institution cannot establish that additional transfers

US-005: Apply the 60-calendar-day periodic statement notification rule
User story

As a disputes analyst, I want the platform to evaluate whether an unauthorized EFT was reported within 60 calendar days after the periodic statement was sent so that the bank can determine whether subsequent unauthorized transfers may be attributable to delayed notification.

Business rules

Under 12 CFR § 1005.6(b)(3):
- The relevant starting point is the date the periodic statement was sent.
- The notification deadline is 60 calendar days after the institution sent the statement.
- If the consumer fails to report the unauthorized EFT within that period, potential liability may extend to qualifying subsequent unauthorized transfers occurring after the 60-day period and before notification.
- The institution must establish that the additional transfers would not have occurred had the consumer notified the institution within the prescribed period.
- This rule does not impose liability for the original unauthorized transfer merely because it was not reported within 60 days.

      transfers under the delayed notification provision

  Scenario: Consumer reports after 60 calendar days
    Given an unauthorized EFT appears on a periodic statement
    And the periodic statement was sent to the consumer
    And the consumer reports the unauthorized EFT after 60 calendar days
    When the platform evaluates consumer liability
    Then the platform should identify qualifying subsequent
      unauthorized transfers occurring after the 60-day period
    And before notification
    And flag those transfers for liability assessment

  Scenario: Original unauthorized transfer is reported after 60 days
    Given an unauthorized EFT appeared on a periodic statement
    And the consumer reports the transfer after 60 calendar days
    When the platform evaluates consumer liability

      be assigned consumer liability under the 60-day provision
    And only qualifying subsequent transfers should be considered

  Scenario: Institution cannot establish preventability
    Given the consumer reported the unauthorized EFT after 60 calendar days
    And additional unauthorized transfers occurred after the 60-day period
    And the institution cannot establish that timely notification
      would have prevented those transfers

    Then the additional transfers should not be included

  Scenario: No additional transfers occurred after the 60-day period
    Given the consumer reported the unauthorized EFT after 60 calendar days
    And no qualifying subsequent unauthorized transfers occurred
    When the platform calculates consumer liability
    Then additional consumer liability under the 60-day provision

US-007: Identify extenuating circumstances and extend notification periods
User story

As a disputes analyst, I want to record extenuating circumstances that prevented a consumer from notifying the institution within the prescribed timeframe so that the platform can support reasonable extensions under Regulation E.

Business rules

Under 12 CFR § 1005.6(c), the notification periods must be extended to a reasonable time when circumstances beyond the consumer's control, such as extended travel or hospitalization, prevented timely notification.

Examples include:
- Hospitalization.
- Extended travel.
- Natural disasters or other circumstances preventing access to communication channels.
- Other documented circumstances beyond the consumer's control.
The examples are not exhaustive. The platform should support an individualized assessment rather than automatically accepting or rejecting an exception.

5. Recommended liability decision matrix
The following matrix should be used as the foundation for the rules engine.
Decision condition	Regulatory treatment
No qualifying unauthorized EFT	No liability under this framework
Required §1005.6(a) prerequisites not met	No consumer liability under §1005.6
Loss/theft reported within 2 business days	Lesser of $50 or qualifying pre-notification transfers
Loss/theft reported after 2 business days	Lesser of $500 or the applicable initial and additional qualifying amounts
Additional transfers not proven preventable	Exclude additional amounts
Unauthorized EFT reported within 60 days of statement being sent	No additional liability under the 60-day delayed-notification provision
Unauthorized EFT reported after 60 days	Potential liability for qualifying subsequent transfers after the 60-day period and before notification
Statement not provided within 30 days after transfer	Suspend application of the 60-day liability provision until statement is provided
Qualifying extenuating circumstances	Extend the applicable notification period reasonably
Transfer occurs after institution notification	No consumer liability for that subsequent transfer under the applicable notification framework

Important: The two-business-day and 60-day provisions are separate liability tests. The rules engine must not simply add their calculated amounts together or apply the $500 cap to the 60-day provision. Where both provisions are relevant, the platform must evaluate the applicable facts, statutory conditions, and transaction-level exclusions without double counting.

These expected values assume the other statutory prerequisites are satisfied, no overlapping assessment or other applicable limitation changes the result, and the bank does not provide more favorable treatment.

9. Key business decisions requiring Compliance approval
Before development sign-off, I recommend obtaining documented decisions on these items.

1. Business-day calendar: Confirm the bank's authoritative calendar and how notification deadlines are calculated, including holidays and notification cut-off times.

2. Notification receipt: Confirm when a notification is legally considered received across telephone, online, branch, and other channels.

3. Preventability standard: Define what evidence is sufficient to establish that additional unauthorized transfers would not have occurred with timely notification.

4. Consumer-friendly policy: Determine whether the bank will waive the regulatory maximum liability entirely or apply lower limits in selected scenarios.

5. Extenuating circumstances: Define approval authority, evidence requirements, and the process for extending notification periods.

6. Periodic statement evidence: Identify the authoritative source for statement sent dates and how missing or delayed statements are handled.

7. Overlapping rules: Establish the approved calculation hierarchy where both the two-business-day and 60-day provisions may apply to a dispute.

8. Manual overrides: Define approval levels, dual-control requirements, and audit expectations.

My recommendation as a business analyst: Do not release the liability calculation engine without the disclosure validation, preventability evidence, and explainable calculation capabilities. These are essential controls, not optional enhancements.

The most important design principle is to keep three values separate:
1. Regulatory maximum liability – what Regulation E permits the bank to hold the consumer liable for.
2. Bank policy liability – what the bank's approved consumer protection policy actually allows it to assess.
3. Final consumer liability – the amount ultimately determined after applying evidence, exceptions, and approved policy.
This separation will make the platform easier to audit, test, maintain, and adapt to future regulatory changes.

This is a business-analysis requirements baseline, not a legal opinion. Final acceptance criteria and calculation logic should be validated by the bank's Regulation E Compliance and Legal teams against the current regulation and applicable interpretations.
